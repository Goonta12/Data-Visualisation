Last updated: 13 October 2026

"Note": this page file I used AI inorder to make it

1. WHICH CHART USES WHICH DATA, AND WHY
=======================================

Chart 1: Advertising overtook graphic design
  Idiom:      Bump chart
  Spec file:  specs/chart1_bump_activity_rank.json
  Data file:  data/design_participation.csv
  Why:        The ranking of activities changes over time, and a bump chart
              shows rank changes directly.

Chart 2: Who does design work (men and women, three survey years)
  Idiom:      Butterfly chart
  Spec file:  specs/chart2_butterfly_gender.json
  Data file:  data/participation_by_gender.csv
  Why:        Two groups compared on the same categories, one panel per year.

Chart 3: Where the businesses are
  Idiom:      Heatmap with bar chart
  Spec file:  specs/chart3_heatmap_bars_per_capita.json
  Data files: data/design_businesses_by_state_2001.csv
              data/state_population_2001.csv
  Why:        The grid compares industries across states per person. The bars
              add the three industries into one total for each state.

Chart 4: Advertising grew the most
  Idiom:      Lollipop chart
  Spec file:  specs/chart4_lollipop_growth.json
  Data file:  data/design_participation.csv
  Why:        One growth figure per activity (2001 to 2007), easy to compare.

Chart 5: Women's share rose in graphic design and fell in architecture
  Idiom:      Slope chart
  Spec file:  specs/chart5_slope_women_share.json
  Data file:  data/participation_by_sex_all.csv
  Why:        Five activities, two dates. Slopes show which way each share moved,
              and the dashed line marks equal numbers of men and women.
              Share = women / (men + women), calculated in the spec.

Chart 6: Men are far more likely to earn $40,000 or more from design
  Idiom:      Connected dot plot
  Spec file:  specs/chart6_connected_dot_income.json
  Data file:  data/income_from_design_by_sex.csv
  Why:        Two groups (men, women) compared in each income band; the line
              between the dots is the gap. One panel for 2001 and one for 2007.

Chart 7: Advertising agencies employ mostly people in non-design jobs
  Idiom:      Treemap (a Vega spec, not Vega-Lite)
  Spec file:  specs/chart7_treemap_occupations.json
  Data file:  data/census2011_occupations_by_industry.csv
  Why:        Parts of a whole in two levels: industry, then occupation. Area
              shows how many people. Vega-Lite cannot draw treemaps, so this
              one uses Vega.

Chart 8: What design adds to the economy
  Idiom:      Waffle chart (two panels)
  Spec file:  specs/chart8_waffle_value_added.json
  Data file:  data/design_value_added_ip_australia.csv
  Why:        Each square is 1% of a group's value added, so shares are easy to
              read and compare between design industries and embedded designers.
              Squares come from rounded cumulative shares so each panel sums to 100.

Chart 9: About two thirds of design businesses are in NSW and Victoria
  Idiom:      Proportional symbol map
  Spec file:  specs/chart9_symbol_map_businesses_2012.json
  Data files: data/design_businesses_by_state_2012.csv
              data/state_labels.csv (circle and label positions, made by me)
              data/australia_states.geojson (boundaries)
  Why:        Circle area shows how many businesses each state has, and colour
              shows its largest industry. Totals are calculated in the spec.

Chart 10: Architecture thrives in Victoria and WA; advertising and other design peak in NSW
  Idiom:      Choropleth map, three panels (small multiples)
  Spec file:  specs/chart10_choropleth_per_capita_2012.json
  Data files: data/design_businesses_by_state_long.csv
              data/state_population_2012.csv
              data/state_labels.csv, data/australia_states.geojson
  Why:        Raw counts mostly reflect population. Dividing by population
              (calculated in the spec) shows which states have more businesses
              for their size. One map per industry on a shared colour scale.

Chart 11: South Australia gained architects; Tasmania and the NT lost advertisers
  Idiom:      Tile grid map, two panels
  Spec file:  specs/chart11_tile_map_change_2007_2012.json
  Data files: data/design_businesses_by_state_long.csv
              data/state_tiles.csv (tile positions, made by me)
  Why:        Equal-sized tiles let the ACT and Tasmania be read as easily as
              Queensland. Colour shows percentage change 2007 to 2012, calculated
              in the spec. Only architecture and advertising are shown because
              the third industry's definition changed between the two years.

Chart 12: Dense and growing: Victoria stands out
  Idiom:      Bivariate choropleth map
  Spec file:  specs/chart12_bivariate_map_density_growth.json
  Data files: data/design_businesses_by_state_long.csv
              data/state_population_2012.csv
              data/state_labels.csv, data/australia_states.geojson
  Why:        Shows two measures at once: businesses per 100,000 residents (2012)
              and growth since 2007, for architecture and advertising combined.
              The class cut-offs (85 and 110 per 100,000; 4% and 8% growth) are
              my own choice.

Chart 13: Design skills cluster around the capital cities
  Idiom:      Dot map (dot density)
  Spec file:  specs/chart13_dot_map_design_qualifications.json
  Data files: data/design_qualification_dots_2016.csv, data/lga_boundaries_2016.geojson
  Why:        One dot is about 250 residents with a qualification in architecture
              and building (blue) or creative arts (orange), so clusters show
              where the skills are. The dots are made from the LGA counts in
              data/lga_design_qualifications_2016.csv.
  Note:       Dot positions are random inside each local government area (seeded
              so the file does not change). Counts are real, positions are not.

Chart 14: Creative arts training is most common in the Sydney and Melbourne areas
  Idiom:      Bin map (hexagon bins)
  Spec file:  specs/chart14_hex_bin_map_creative_share.json
  Data files: data/creative_arts_hexbins_2016.geojson, data/australia_states.geojson
  Why:        Local government areas vary hugely in size, which makes a plain
              choropleth hard to read. Equal-sized hexagons (about 80 km) give
              every part of the country the same visual weight.
  Note:       Hexagons were calculated by me: each LGA's centre point is assigned
              to a hexagon on an equal-area grid and the LGA counts are added up.
              Hexagons with fewer than 2,000 qualified residents are left grey.
              City dots and names in the spec are plain coordinates.

Chart 15: Creative arts graduates favour the inner city
  Idiom:      Choropleth map at local government area level (three panels)
  Spec file:  specs/chart15_choropleth_creative_share_metros.json
  Data files: data/lga_boundaries_2016.geojson
              data/lga_design_qualifications_2016.csv
  Why:        A rate (creative arts qualifications as a share of all qualified
              residents), so big and small areas are comparable. Close-ups of
              Sydney and Melbourne show the inner-city pattern that the national
              map hides.

Chart 16: Inner cities lean creative; the outer suburbs lean towards building
  Idiom:      Proportional symbol map (three city panels)
  Spec file:  specs/chart16_symbol_map_metro_lgas.json
  Data files: data/lga_boundaries_2016.geojson, data/lga_centroids.csv,
              data/lga_design_qualifications_2016.csv
  Why:        Circle size is the number of residents in the two fields and colour
              is the creative arts share of those two fields (orange creative,
              blue building). Circle positions are LGA centre points that I
              calculated from the boundary file.

Advanced idioms: bump, butterfly, heatmap + bars, lollipop, slope, connected
dot plot, treemap, waffle, proportional symbol map (twice), choropleth map (twice),
tile grid map, bivariate map, dot map and bin map = 16 charts, with 6 map idioms
(symbol, choropleth, tile grid, bivariate, dot, bin) across 8 map charts. Check the
assignment for which idioms count as "advanced".

A flow map was not made: I could not find real origin-destination flow data for
design (for example people or work moving between places) that I could read from
here. If you find one, a flow map would fit section 5 or 7.

  6. Zooming in on the cities ................ charts 15 (choropleth), 16 (symbol map)
  7. The people behind the businesses ........ charts 1 (bump), 4 (lollipop), 2 (butterfly),
                                               5 (slope), 6 (connected dot plot)
  8. Inside the industry ..................... chart 7 (treemap)


2. DATA FILES
=============

design_participation.csv
  Holds:   People who did design work in the 12 months to April: graphic design,
           advertising, fashion design, other design (2001, 2004, 2007).
  Source:  ABS Survey of Work in Selected Culture and Leisure Activities,
           published in ABS 4172.0 Arts and Culture in Australia.

participation_by_gender.csv
  Holds:   Graphic, fashion and other design split by sex (older file, used by
           chart 2). Men in fashion design are flagged as a high margin of error.
  Source:  Same ABS survey.

participation_by_sex_all.csv   (NEW)
  Holds:   All five activities (graphic design, fashion design, architecture,
           advertising, other design) by sex, 2001, 2004, 2007, in people.
  Source:  Same ABS survey: 2001 and 2004 editions, and 4172.0 (2009) Table 15.4
           for 2007. Published in thousands; multiplied by 1,000.
  Note:    Components do not add to the total number of people because some
           people did more than one activity.
  Correction: an earlier note said advertising is not split by sex. It is, in these
           tables. Chart 2 still leaves it out, but it could be added.

income_from_design_by_sex.csv   (NEW)
  Holds:   Paid design workers by annual income from design, by sex, 2001 and 2007.
  Source:  Same ABS survey, Table 15.4 in the 2001 and 2009 editions.
  Notes:   The 2001 tables have five bands ($5,000 to $19,999 and $20,000 to
           $39,999 are separate); I added those two so they match 2007's
           "$5,000 to $39,999". People paid nothing (2001) and goods-and-services-only
           (2007) are left out. Dollar bands are not adjusted for inflation.
           Women $40,000+ in 2001 has a high margin of error (RSE 25 to 50%).
           I did not use the 2004 table: its published persons totals for two
           bands do not match males plus females.

census2011_occupations_by_industry.csv   (NEW)
  Holds:   People employed in architectural services, advertising services and
           other specialised design services, by occupation (2011 Census main job).
  Source:  ABS 4172.0 (2014 edition), Census 2011.
  Notes:   "Not stated or not adequately described" is calculated by me:
           published all-occupations total minus cultural minus non-cultural.
           The ABS randomly adjusted small cells.

design_value_added_ip_australia.csv   (NEW, second publisher)
  Holds:   Value added ($ millions, constant 2019 dollars) by design industry,
           and by designers working in other industries ("embedded designers").
  Source:  IP Australia, Defining design: Design's role in the Australian economy
           (2020), Table 1, p. 10. IP Australia uses BCAR (2018), IBISWorld (2019)
           and the Census.
  Notes:   Design industries: average 2008 to 2016. Embedded designers: average of
           2011 and 2016, which IP Australia calls experimental.
           The report gives $61.1bn (3.4% of GDP) for design industries and about
           $67.5bn (more than 3.5%) with embedded designers.

lga_design_qualifications_2016.csv   (NEW)
  Holds:   Residents (persons) by field of their non-school qualification, by local
           government area (LGA 2016): architecture and building, creative arts, and
           the total of all fields. 543 rows.
  Source:  ABS Census of Population and Housing 2016, table G47 "Non-school
           Qualification: Field of Study by age by sex (LGA)", read from the ABS Data
           API (dataflow ABS_C16_G47_LGA; ages: total; persons).
  Notes:   The API marks this dataflow as non-production. I spot-checked Sydney,
           Moreland, Brisbane and Stirling with separate single-LGA queries and all
           values matched. National totals: 633,308 architecture and building,
           401,161 creative arts, out of 11,412,288 with a qualification (3.5% creative arts).
           "Total" includes every field plus inadequately described and not stated.
           The ABS names the merged Cootamundra-Gundagai area "Gundagai (A)".
           Botany Bay (C) and Rockdale (C) are added together (coded BAYSIDE_NSW)
           to match the boundary file's Bayside. The ACT has no LGAs, so its whole
           value is the row coded 89399. Broome (S) has no creative arts value.
           "No usual address" and "Migratory" rows are left out. The ABS says field
           of study is a broad classification: architecture and building includes
           building trades, and creative arts includes music, visual and performing arts.

lga_boundaries_2016.geojson   (NEW, map)
  Holds:   Local government area boundaries, simplified (finer detail inside the five
           city windows). Properties: code, name, state, metro (my city window).
  Source:  geoBoundaries (wmgeolab), ADM2 simplified, Australia, open licence.
           https://github.com/wmgeolab/geoBoundaries
  Notes:   geoBoundaries has some post-2016 names; I matched them to the 2016 ABS names
           by hand (for example Dubbo = Western Plains Regional). The "metro" windows
           are boxes around Sydney, Melbourne, Brisbane, Adelaide and Perth, not
           official Greater Capital City boundaries.

lga_centroids.csv, design_qualification_dots_2016.csv, creative_arts_hexbins_2016.geojson   (NEW, calculated by me)
  Holds:   LGA centre points; the dot map's dots (1 dot is about 250 people,
           placed randomly inside each LGA); and the hexagon sums.
  Source:  Calculated from the two files above. See the notes on charts 13 to 16.

design_businesses_by_state_2007.csv   (NEW)
  Holds:   Businesses by state for architectural services, advertising services,
           and commercial art and display services, June 2007.
  Source:  ABS Counts of Australian Businesses, Table 15.1 in ABS 4172.0 (2009).
  Note:    State figures add exactly to the published national totals.

design_businesses_by_state_long.csv   (NEW)
  Holds:   The 2007 and 2012 state counts in one long table (year, state,
           industry, businesses), used by charts 10 to 12. "Other design services"
           is commercial art and display (2007) or other specialised design (2012).
           These are different industries, so only architecture and advertising
           are compared between the two years.
  Source:  The two files above.

state_population_2012.csv   (NEW)
  Holds:   State population at end of June 2012 in thousands, and in people
           (thousands x 1,000).
  Source:  ABS Australian Demographic Statistics 3101.0, June 2012 issue,
           June key figures. The figures are marked preliminary.

australia_states.geojson, state_tiles.csv, state_labels.csv   (maps)
  Holds:   State boundaries (Natural Earth admin-1, public domain, filtered to
           Australia, simplified), tile positions and label positions.
  Note:    The tile and label positions are my own layout, not data.

design_businesses_by_state_2001.csv
  Holds:   Businesses in architectural services, advertising services, and
           commercial art and display services, by state, June 2001.
  Source:  ABS Australian Business Register counts, published in ABS 4172.0.

design_businesses_by_state_2012.csv   (NEW; state codes used from now)
  Holds:   Businesses by state: architectural services, advertising services,
           other specialised design services, June 2012.
  Source:  ABS Counts of Australian Businesses, in ABS 4172.0 (2014 edition).
  Note:    States do not add to the national total because some businesses have
           no state recorded.

design_businesses_by_size_2012.csv   (NEW)
  Holds:   2012 businesses by employment size (non-employing, 1 to 19, 20 to 199,
           200+) for the same three industries.
  Source:  Same.
  Note:    The page prints 14,442 for other specialised design services, but its
           parts add to 14,342, and so do its state figures, so 14,342 is used.

design_businesses_national.csv, architectural_services_employment.csv,
creative_arts_students.csv, paid_participation_2010_11.csv, state_population_2001.csv
  Holds:   National business counts (2001, 2007, 2012); architectural services
           employment (Census 2001, 2011); creative arts university students
           (2003 to 2007); paid participation in design-related activities 2010-11;
           state population June 2001.
  Source:  ABS (see links). Persons population = males + females (added by me).
  Note:    2001 business counts and 2007/2012 counts use different counting bases.
           Creative arts students includes music, visual arts and media.

4. KNOWN LIMITS (mention these in your write-up)
================================================

- Charts 13 to 16 use Census 2016 data. They are broader fields of study, not
  designers, and the API dataflow is marked non-production (see above).
- Maps 9 to 12 are at state level because the design-specific counts are only published
  for states. The 2012 population figures are preliminary. State figures for
  small places (ACT, NT, Tasmania) change a lot with a few businesses.
- The newest design-specific data is from 2012 (businesses) and 2011 (Census). The
  assignment asks for the most recent data. The Census 2021 and Jobs and Skills
  Australia occupation profiles are the next places to look; I could not open
  those pages from here.
- Two publishers are used (ABS and IP Australia). IP Australia's value added
  figures draw on BCAR and IBISWorld as well as the Census.
- Survey participation counts people who did any design activity, including
  unpaid, so they are not the same as design jobs.
- The 2007 income bands and the 2001 bands only match after merging two 2001 bands,
  and they are not adjusted for inflation.
- The treemap's "Other occupations" group mixes non-cultural occupations with
  a small not-stated group.
- Advertising is missing from the butterfly chart (chart 2), although the tables
  do split it by sex.


5. USING THE ONLINE EDITOR
==========================

Files in online_editor/ have the data pasted inside, so they work when pasted into
https://vega.github.io/editor. Charts 13 to 16 are not included because their
boundary files are too large for the editor; run them from the project folder. For chart 7 choose "Vega" at the top of the editor
instead of "Vega-Lite".
